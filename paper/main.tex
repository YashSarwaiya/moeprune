\begin{abstract}
Expert pruning of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language models is believed to be
safe only up to roughly 25\% without retraining. We show that this ceiling is
an artifact of demanding \emph{general} capability, and that when only one
domain must survive, an off-the-shelf fine-grained MoE tolerates far deeper
cuts. On Qwen3-30B-A3B (128 experts/layer, top-8), a norm-aware contribution
score computed from a teacher-forced prefill over only ${\sim}30$ domain
demonstrations retains 97\% of baseline HumanEval pass@1 with 50\% of routed
experts removed, and 90\% with 65\% removed---while GSM8K collapses from
99.3\% to 33.3\%, confirming genuinely domain-targeted compression. A
size-matched random control scores 26.8\% HumanEval at the 50\% ratio, an
advantage of 62 points for informed selection. We further show that
domain-pruned variants \emph{compose}: the per-layer union of independently
derived coding and math expert sets (52\% of experts) scores within 1--2
points of the full model on both HumanEval (90.9\%) and GSM8K (97.3\%), while
dropping 34 points on a non-STEM MMLU probe---a two-skill specialist
assembled post hoc with no retraining. Composition holds for distant domain
pairs (code$\cup$humanities: 84.1\% HumanEval, 74.3\% MMLU-humanities), whose
keep-sets overlap less than sibling pairs. Finally, we observe a sharp
asymmetry: procedural domains (coding, math) prune cleanly, but a knowledge
domain (humanities) does not---the humanities-only specialist collapses even
on its own domain (28.7\% vs.\ 84.7\% baseline), yet unions restore it to
74.3\%. Our results suggest fine-grained MoE models are post-hoc composable at
the expert level, and motivate applying the recipe to the most fine-grained
public MoE, Kimi K3 (896 experts/layer).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts architectures dominate the largest open language
models, but their checkpoints are monolithic: a user who needs only coding
ability must nevertheless store and serve every expert, including those that
exist to support poetry, law, and the long tail of world knowledge. One-shot
expert pruning offers relief, but published results converge on a ceiling near
25\% of experts before general performance degrades \cite{heapr,mone,moei2},
with ${\sim}50\%$ reachable only with retraining or distillation
\cite{moepruner}.

We ask a different question: \textbf{how much of the expert pool is needed if
the user cares about a single domain?} This reframing removes the constraint
that produced the 25\% ceiling---capability outside the target domain is
deliberately sacrificed rather than preserved. Building on few-shot expert
localization \cite{easyep}, we prune an off-the-shelf fine-grained MoE with a
simple training-free pipeline and measure the full quality-versus-ratio curve,
with controls the one-shot pruning literature typically omits.

Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A domain-restricted pruning curve far above the general
ceiling.} With a norm-aware contribution score over ${\sim}30$ demonstrations
plus protection of ``super experts'' identified on a general set, coding
capability survives 50\% expert removal at 89.0\% HumanEval pass@1 (baseline
91.5\%) and 65\% removal at 82.3\%. The curve holds a plateau to its knee at
${\sim}65\%$, decays steadily to 80\% (45.1\%), and collapses by 87.5\%.

\item \textbf{Matched random controls quantifying the value of selection.} At
identical keep-set sizes, random expert selection scores 26.8\% vs.\ 89.0\%
(50\% ratio) and 0.6\% vs.\ 82.3\% (65\% ratio). Informed selection---not
redundancy---carries the result.

\item \textbf{Training-free composition of domain specialists.} The per-layer
union of independently derived coding and math keep-sets (67/128 experts, 75\%
of the sum of the parts) scores 90.9\% HumanEval and 97.3\% GSM8K---above both
specialists and within 2 points of the unpruned model---while a non-STEM MMLU
probe confirms it remains a specialist (50.3\% vs.\ 84.7\% baseline). A distant
pair (code$\cup$humanities) also composes.

\item \textbf{A skills/knowledge asymmetry.} Identical machinery that yields
strong coding and math specialists fails to produce a humanities specialist
(28.7\% on MMLU-humanities vs.\ 84.7\% baseline), consistent with reports that
semantic knowledge is distributed while procedural skill is localized
\cite{whatgets,illusion}. Unions partially restore the lost capability
(74.3\%), indicating the deficit is complementary rather than absolute.
\end{enumerate}

All experiments are one-shot (no gradient updates), run on a single NVIDIA
B200 GPU, and use masking that is exactly output-equivalent to checkpoint
surgery (Section~\ref{sec:mask}), making the study reproducible at minimal
cost.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{General expert pruning.} HEAPr \cite{heapr} and MoNE \cite{mone}
achieve near-lossless one-shot pruning at 20--25\% on DeepSeek/Qwen MoE
families; MoNE reports ${\sim}24$--25\% as the maximum within 1\% loss for
Qwen3-30B-A3B---the same base model we use, providing a direct published
anchor. MoE-I$^2$ \cite{moei2} and MoE-Pruner \cite{moepruner} reach
${\sim}50\%$ only with intra-expert decomposition, fine-tuning, or expert-wise
distillation. Scoring criteria for one-shot pruning are surveyed in
\cite{scoring}; AIMER \cite{aimer} removes calibration dependence in the
task-agnostic setting. Our setting differs: we do not preserve general
capability.

\paragraph{Domain-specific pruning.} EASY-EP \cite{easyep} introduced few-shot
expert localization with output-aware importance, which our contribution score
follows in spirit. C-PRUNE \cite{cprune} clusters experts for task-specific
compression in the biomedical domain, and recent work reports coding-domain
pruning at ${\sim}50\%$ on earlier-generation MoEs \cite{halfexperts}. Relative
to this line we contribute (i)~the full quality-vs-ratio curve with its knee,
(ii)~matched random controls, and (iii)~the composition results, none of which
appear in prior domain-pruning studies to our knowledge.

\paragraph{Expert merging and modularity.} An alternative to deletion is
merging similar experts \cite{hcsmoe,reap}; REAP \cite{reap} finds pruning
superior for one-shot compression. EMO \cite{emo} pretrains MoEs so that
task-specific expert subsets can be selected at deployment, arguing that
standard MoEs specialize syntactically rather than semantically. Our results
provide a counterpoint: a standard, conventionally trained MoE already supports
post-hoc subset selection and recombination for procedural domains with no
pretraining modification---though EMO-style training may be what closes the gap
we observe on knowledge domains. Analyses of expert specialization
\cite{whatgets,illusion,doexist} motivate our protection of disproportionately
active super experts.

\section{Method}

Given a routed-MoE checkpoint with $E$ experts per layer and top-$k$ routing,
the pipeline has four training-free stages.

\subsection{Routing capture}
We run a teacher-forced prefill (no generation) over a small calibration set;
each item is a prompt concatenated with a reference solution. Hooks on each
layer's router record, per token, the top-$k$ expert indices and their
renormalized gate weights, and per expert the $L_2$ norm of its output. Only
dense per-(layer, expert) accumulators are stored.

\subsection{Norm-aware contribution scoring}
Routing frequency conflates syntactic regularity with importance, so we score
expert $e$ in layer $l$ as the accumulated gate-weighted output norm
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{score}(l,e) \;=\; \sum_{t} g_{l,e}(x_t)\,\bigl\lVert f_{l,e}(x_t)
\bigr\rVert_2 ,
\end{equation}
over the domain calibration set---the contribution the expert actually adds to
the residual stream, following output-aware importance \cite{easyep}.

\subsection{Super-expert protection}
A small set of experts does disproportionate work regardless of domain
\cite{illusion}. On a \emph{general} calibration set we flag experts whose gate
mass exceeds $8\times$ the layer median, or that fall in the top 2\% of a
layer's mass, and include them in every keep-set (6.5 experts/layer for our
model). Domain pruning must not delete generic infrastructure.

\subsection{Pruning, masking, and composition}
\label{sec:mask}
A \emph{manifest} lists surviving experts per layer: the protected set plus the
top domain-scored experts up to the target ratio, with a floor of $2k$ experts
per layer. For evaluation we \emph{mask} rather than rewrite: a hook sets
pruned experts' router logits to $-\infty$, which is exactly output-equivalent
to deleting the expert tensors and slicing the router (verified to
$<10^{-5}$ relative error on a synthetic model with planted structure). Masking
makes a sweep cost zero storage; deployment uses real checkpoint surgery with
identical outputs.

\textbf{Composition} is the per-layer set union of two manifests. No scores are
recomputed and no calibration is rerun; the union inherits both protected sets.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Model.} Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507: 48 layers, 128 routed experts
per layer, top-8 routing with renormalization, bf16, evaluated on one NVIDIA
B200 with greedy decoding throughout.

\paragraph{Calibration sets.} Authored for this study; no benchmark items are
reused. Coding: 30 prompt-and-solution items across eight languages
(roughly 15k tokens, teacher-forced). Math: 28 worked word and algebra
problems, disjoint from GSM8K.
Humanities: 24 essay-style answers spanning history, philosophy, law, and the
arts. General: 16 mixed items, used only for super-expert protection.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.} HumanEval pass@1 (164 problems, code-fence extraction,
sandboxed subprocess execution); GSM8K exact-match on a fixed 150-problem
subset; and a knowledge probe of 300 questions sampled from six non-STEM MMLU
subjects (philosophy, world religions, jurisprudence, European history,
marketing, prehistory). Baselines: 91.5\% HumanEval, 99.3\% GSM8K, 84.7\%
MMLU-hum. At these sample sizes, differences under ${\sim}2$--3 points are
within noise; we interpret only larger gaps.

\paragraph{Ratios.} $\{25, 50, 65, 70, 75, 80, 87.5\}\%$ of routed experts
removed. The 87.5\% point coincides with the $2k$-per-layer floor (16 kept, 8
active).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Domain-restricted pruning beats the general ceiling}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
\toprule
Experts removed & Kept/layer & HumanEval & GSM8K \\
\midrule
0\%    & 128 & 91.5 & 99.3 \\
25\%   &  96 & 91.5 & 97.3 \\
50\%   &  64 & 89.0 & 94.0 \\
65\%   &  45 & 82.3 & 33.3 \\
70\%   &  38 & 70.7 &  6.0 \\
75\%   &  32 & 63.4 &  2.0 \\
80\%   &  26 & 45.1 &  0.0 \\
87.5\% &  16 &  0.0 &  0.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Coding-domain pruning sweep. In-domain (HumanEval) capability holds a
plateau to ${\sim}65\%$ while out-of-domain (GSM8K) collapses much earlier.}
\end{table}

Coding-targeted pruning holds HumanEval within 2.5 points at 50\% removal
---double the published general-pruning ceiling for this model \cite{mone}---and
within 10 points at 65\%, where GSM8K has already fallen from 99.3 to 33.3. The
in-domain curve is not smooth to the end: a plateau to the knee at
${\sim}65\%$, steady decay through 80\%, then collapse. The 87.5\% point sits at
the routing-slack floor and likely reflects that floor as much as the selection
method.

Routing statistics foreshadow the curve: on the general set, mean Gini of
per-layer expert mass is 0.63, ${\sim}19/128$ experts carry 50\% of layer mass,
and ${\sim}65/128$ carry 95\%---half the pool shares the last 5\% of routing
mass.

\subsection{Selection, not redundancy: matched random controls}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Keep policy & Kept/layer & HumanEval & GSM8K & MMLU-hum \\
\midrule
domain-scored (50\%)   & 64 & 89.0 & 94.0 & --- \\
random (50\%)          & 64 & 26.8 & 40.0 & --- \\
domain-scored (65\%)   & 45 & 82.3 & 33.3 & --- \\
random (65\%)          & 45 &  0.6 & 16.0 & --- \\
union code$\cup$math   & 67 & 90.9 & 97.3 & 50.3 \\
random size-matched    & 67 & 58.5 & 37.3 & 62.3 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Random selection at matched keep-set sizes. Informed selection wins
in-domain by 32--82 points, but \emph{loses} on the neutral domain.}
\end{table}

Random selection at matched sizes loses 62 points of HumanEval at the 50\%
ratio and 82 points at 65\%. At 67/layer the pattern inverts on the neutral
domain: random \emph{beats} the union on MMLU-hum (62.3 vs.\ 50.3) while
trailing by 32--60 points in-domain. Random preserves diffuse mediocrity;
informed selection concentrates capability into the chosen domains. (Random
keep-sets here do not receive super-expert protection; isolating protection
from domain scoring is left as an ablation.)

\subsection{Domain specialists compose post hoc}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Variant & Kept/layer & HumanEval & GSM8K & MMLU-hum \\
\midrule
code-only (65\%)          &  45 & 82.3 & 33.3 & --- \\
math-only (65\%)          &  45 &  0.0 & 93.3 & --- \\
\textbf{code$\cup$math}   &  67 & \textbf{90.9} & \textbf{97.3} & 50.3 \\
hum-only (65\%)           &  45 &  0.0 &  0.7 & 28.7 \\
\textbf{code$\cup$hum}    &  71 & \textbf{84.1} & 32.7 & \textbf{74.3} \\
baseline                  & 128 & 91.5 & 99.3 & 84.7 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Composition by per-layer set union. Unions exceed their parts
in-domain while remaining specialists on the neutral probe.}
\end{table}

The code and math keep-sets share 22.6 experts/layer, so their union is 67
rather than 90 (75\% of the sum of parts). The union exceeds \emph{both}
specialists on their own domains and lands within 1--2 points of the unpruned
model on both benchmarks at 52\% of the experts, while its 34-point deficit on
the non-STEM probe confirms it remains a two-domain specialist rather than a
recovered generalist. Composition is not limited to sibling domains: code and
humanities keep-sets overlap less (19/layer), and their union still scores 84.1
HumanEval and 74.3 MMLU-hum while GSM8K---covered by neither set---stays at
specialist level (32.7).

The specialists are strikingly disjoint in capability despite sharing half
their experts: the math-only model solves zero HumanEval problems, while the
code-only model retains residual GSM8K (33.3), plausibly via shared
arithmetic-adjacent machinery.

\subsection{Skills prune; knowledge does not}

The same pipeline that produces strong coding (82.3) and math (93.3)
specialists at the 65\% ratio \emph{fails} for humanities: the hum-only variant
scores 28.7 on its own domain, barely above the 25\% multiple-choice floor.
This is consistent with analyses finding procedural competence concentrated in
few experts while semantic knowledge is spread across many
\cite{whatgets,illusion}. Notably, the code$\cup$hum union recovers humanities
to 74.3---capability neither part exhibits alone, with code-only presumably
contributing generic instruction-following and composition machinery. Two
implications follow: (i)~domain-restricted compression is substantially more
effective for skill-like than knowledge-like domains at matched ratios;
(ii)~deficits from aggressive pruning can be complementary, and unions repair
them cheaply.

We flag one confound: the humanities calibration set is essay-style while the
probe is multiple-choice, so part of the hum-only collapse may reflect format
sensitivity. A format-matched ablation is future work.

\section{Limitations}

Single model, single size, and a single seed per point; benchmark sample sizes
give ${\pm}2$--3 point noise. HumanEval and GSM8K are older benchmarks with
likely training contamination, so our claims rest on \emph{relative} retention
under identical decoding rather than absolute scores. Masking realizes quality
equivalence but not memory or latency savings, which require the mechanically
verified surgery path. The 87.5\% point is confounded by the routing-slack
floor. Random controls omit super-expert protection. EMO \cite{emo} achieves
related composability via modified pretraining; we do not claim post-hoc
selection matches purpose-trained modularity, only that useful composability
already exists in standard checkpoints.

\section{Future Work}

The natural target is Kimi K3 (896 experts/layer, top-16, 2.8T parameters,
released July 2026)---$7\times$ finer expert granularity than studied here. If
specialization grows with granularity \cite{easyep}, the knee should move
right, and domain-restricted compression of the strongest open model would
yield deployable specialists in the 200--400\,GB range from a 1.56\,TB
checkpoint. Further directions: knowledge-domain pruning with format-matched
calibration, per-layer adaptive ratios, expert-wise distillation to recover the
post-knee region, and $N$-way unions (``models \`a la carte'').
